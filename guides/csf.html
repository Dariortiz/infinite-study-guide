Computing Foundations · infinite study guide

Darío's CSF MOOC

Know what can be computed—and at what numerical cost.

Assembled from two spines that don't otherwise share a syllabus: Chris Rycroft's Harvard AM205 lectures for conditioning, stability, and stable numerical algorithms, and Michael Sipser's MIT 18.404J for automata through NP-completeness, with standalone talks — Nachum Dershowitz on the Church–Turing thesis, Kayvon Fatahalian's Stanford CS149 on parallelism — filling gaps neither course covers, backed by Trefethen–Bau, Sipser's own textbook, and Arora–Barak. The order runs numerical error analysis first, then automata, computability, and complexity in Sipser's own sequence, with Markov-chain and parallel-computing extras held back until a later project actually needs them.

Choose your route

There is no single course that runs from floating-point conditioning through automata, computability, and NP-completeness without swapping formalism twice. Rycroft's Harvard AM205 lectures anchor the numerical half; Sipser's MIT 18.404J carries the theory-of-computation half end to end, and Trefethen–Bau and Arora–Barak backstop both in writing.

EXP

Computing explorer

Take Rycroft's conditioning-and-stability sequence plus Sipser's core automata, computability, and complexity units through NP-completeness.

12–16 h video + reading
BLD

Balanced builder

Complete every core unit and exit artifact: the numerical-error report, the DFA/PDA interpreters, the reducibility dossier, and a full NP-completeness proof.

24–32 h + reading
DIP

Deep dive

Add Sipser's space-complexity and hierarchy-theorem lectures with Arora–Barak in full, then take either Metropolis–Hastings through Stan's diagnostics or Fatahalian's parallelism sequence through measured scaling.

38–55 h + reading

Capstones & exit criteria

A finished route ends in an artifact you can run and a statement you can defend.

Verified numerical pipeline

  1. Choose a numerical problem with a known reference solution or invariant.
  2. Implement two algorithms and separate conditioning from stability.
  3. Package tests, environment metadata, seeds, and a reproducible report.

Reduction and solver study

  1. Prove an NP-completeness result with full encoding-size analysis.
  2. Implement the reduction and validate it on generated instances.
  3. Benchmark a solver while stating clearly what worst-case theory does not predict.

Weekly loop

  • Watch: separate the algorithm from the correctness or complexity claim it supports.
  • Derive: reprove one bound, reduction, or hierarchy result by hand.
  • Build: implement one interpreter, reduction, or numerical algorithm.
  • Explain: state exactly what the theorem does and does not predict about real performance.

Minimum portfolio

  • Numerical experiment report separating model, discretization, conditioning, and floating-point error.
  • DFA and PDA interpreters with one proved language classification.
  • A reduction dossier with a computable transformation and a full iff proof.
  • A complete NP-completeness proof with a solver benchmark against the theorem's actual predictions.

Exit criteria

Scientific computing
You can separate conditioning from algorithmic stability, implement Householder QR, and report forward error, backward error, and residual as distinct quantities.
Automata
You can construct and minimize a DFA, prove non-regularity with the pumping lemma or Myhill–Nerode, and build a PDA equivalent to a context-free grammar.
Computability
You can design a Turing machine at implementation-description level, prove undecidability by diagonalization, and write a mapping reduction with a complete iff proof.
Complexity
You can write a verifier, prove NP-completeness with full encoding-size analysis, and state exactly what a hierarchy theorem does and does not separate.
MCMC & HPC extras
You can implement Metropolis–Hastings, diagnose chain convergence with autocorrelation and effective sample size, and predict parallel speedup before measuring it — held optional until a downstream project needs it.
