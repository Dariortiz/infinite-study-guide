Darío's TRC MOOC

Learn the trapdoor. Then trust nothing else.

A non-centralized course built from Christof Paar's Introduction to Cryptography lecture series and Boneh and Shoup's free Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography, backed by the NIST and IETF standards each primitive actually ships as. The order follows the field's own history: perfect secrecy and its impossibility, symmetric ciphers composed into authenticated encryption, the number theory public-key crypto needs, then RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic curves as three answers to the same trapdoor problem, closing on the TLS 1.3 handshake that assembles all of it into one deployed protocol. Post-quantum replacements are the PQC guide; computing on encrypted or distributed data is FHE and MPC; this one is the classical toolbox all four assume you already have.

Three eras in three lines: perfect secrecy makes the ciphertext statistically independent of the message, RSA trades that guarantee for a trapdoor almost nobody can invert, and Diffie–Hellman lets two strangers agree on a secret over a channel everyone is listening to.

Prerequisites

Modular arithmetic, basic probability, and a general-purpose programming language — Python is enough for every lab here. No prior cryptography background assumed; that is the point of this guide.

Stay in this guide: this guide owns the classical toolbox — symmetric ciphers, hash functions, MACs, key derivation, password hashing, RSA, Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves, password-authenticated exchange, signatures, and how TLS assembles them. Post-quantum replacements for these same primitives are the PQC guide. Computing on encrypted data without ever decrypting it is FHE. Joint computation across parties who don't trust each other is MPC. Proving a statement without revealing why it's true is ZKP. Session-level ratchets and group messaging are E2E. Hardware isolation is TEE. Chain consensus and settlement is DLT.
MODULE 00

Foundations, Kerckhoffs, and perfect secrecy

Establish that a cipher's security must not depend on secrecy of the algorithm, formalize the one-time pad's perfect secrecy via Shannon's theorem, and show why the OTP is impractical — motivating everything that follows.

Lecture 1: Introduction to Cryptography
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum

Symmetric/asymmetric vocabulary, Kerckhoffs's principle, and why "security through obscurity" fails as an engineering assumption once an algorithm ships.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 3: Stream Ciphers, Random Numbers and the One Time Pad
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum

Builds the one-time pad from XOR with a truly random key stream, then sketches Shannon's proof that ciphertext reveals zero information about plaintext when the key is used once.

Checkpoint: encrypt one 8-bit block under two different random keys and show both plaintexts are equally consistent with the resulting ciphertext — that's perfect secrecy, not a probability argument taken on faith.
▶ YouTube
A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography — Ch. 2, Encryption
Dan Boneh & Victor Shoup · free textbook, §2.1–2.2

Formalizes Shannon ciphers and perfect security (§2.1), proves perfect secrecy forces keys as long as the message (§2.1.3), then relaxes to computational ciphers and semantic security (§2.2) — the standard the rest of this guide assumes.

↗ Read
VENONA: Soviet Espionage and the American Response, 1939–1957
National Security Agency · declassified historical release

Wartime pressure forced Soviet cryptographic centers to reuse thousands of one-time pad pages; NSA cryptanalysts exploited the reused key material to read messages for decades — real-world proof that perfect secrecy dies the moment a key repeats.

MODULE 01

Stream ciphers and pseudorandomness

Distinguish a PRG from true randomness, build LFSR-based stream ciphers and show why they're breakable, and land on a real modern stream cipher (ChaCha20) as the practical answer.

Lecture 4: Stream Ciphers and Linear Feedback Shift Registers
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum

Builds a keystream generator from an LFSR's feedback polynomial and shows why linearity is fatal: roughly 2n known keystream bits let linear algebra reconstruct the entire generator of linear complexity n.

Build: implement an 8-bit LFSR, generate 32 keystream bits, and recover the feedback taps from only 16 of them.
▶ YouTube
Weaknesses in the Key Scheduling Algorithm of RC4
Scott Fluhrer, Itsik Mantin & Adi Shamir · SAC 2001

Identifies classes of RC4 weak keys and a key-scheduling bias strong enough to fully recover WEP keys from ciphertext alone — the paper that ended RC4's credibility and eventually got it banned from TLS (RFC 7465).

↗ Paper
RFC 8439: ChaCha20 and Poly1305 for IETF Protocols
Y. Nir & A. Langley · IETF, June 2018

Specifies ChaCha20 as a counter-mode PRG built from a 20-round ARX (add-rotate-XOR) permutation — no S-boxes, no lookup tables, no data-dependent branches, which is what makes it fast in software and immune to the cache-timing attacks that hit table-based ciphers.

↗ RFC
DSA-1571-1: openssl — predictable random number generator
Debian Security Advisory · CVE-2008-0166, May 2008

A 2006 Debian OpenSSL patch accidentally stripped nearly all entropy from the seed, leaving only about 32,768 possible keypairs per architecture; every key generated on an affected system for two years fell into a scannable, effectively guessable set.

Audit: given a system that seeds its PRNG from a source you don't control, list what you'd verify before trusting a generated key — and what evidence would have caught this failure before it shipped.
↗ Read
MODULE 02

Block ciphers: DES and the Feistel structure

Feistel Cipher
Dr Mike Pound · Computerphile

Shows why a Feistel network only needs its round function to be invertible in one direction — decryption just runs the same rounds in reverse — which is what let DES use S-boxes with no algebraic inverse at all.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 5: Data Encryption Standard (DES): Encryption
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum

Traces DES's 16-round Feistel structure, the expansion permutation, and the eight S-boxes, and explains the design rationale — avalanche and resistance to linear approximation — behind S-box selection.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 6: Data Encryption Standard (DES): Key Schedule and Decryption
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum

Derives the 16 round keys via the PC-1/PC-2 permutations and rotating key halves, then shows why Feistel decryption is just encryption with the round-key order reversed.

Checkpoint: trace one DES round by hand for a toy 8-bit block and identify which S-box property makes differential analysis harder.
▶ YouTube
Exhaustive Cryptanalysis of the NBS Data Encryption Standard
Whitfield Diffie & Martin Hellman · Computer, 1977

Argues DES's 56-bit key is too small against a dedicated cracking machine, and shows why naive double encryption barely helps: a meet-in-the-middle attack breaks two-key double-DES in about 2^57 steps — the result that forced three independent keys (3DES) and, decades later, NIST's 2023 withdrawal of the standard specifying it.

Lecture 8: Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)

Traces one AES round — SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns, AddRoundKey — as a substitution-permutation network rather than a Feistel structure, derives the key schedule, and covers why Rijndael won the NIST AES competition over four other finalists.

NIST · federal standard, updated 2023

The normative specification: exact S-box tables, round counts per key size, and the byte-level algorithm any conforming implementation must match.

Intel Advanced Encryption Standard Instructions (AES-NI)
Intel · developer reference

Six x86 instructions execute AES rounds directly in hardware in data-independent time with no table lookups, closing the cache-timing leakage that plagues naive software S-box implementations.

Cache-timing attacks on AES
Daniel J. Bernstein · April 2005

Recovers a full AES key from a remote OpenSSL server by timing table-lookup-based encryptions alone — no access to plaintext, ciphertext, or the S-box tables themselves, just cache-hit/miss timing variance.

MODULE 04

Modes of operation and authenticated encryption

Understand why a block cipher alone doesn't secure a message longer than one block, trace ECB's visible failure mode, build up through CBC/CTR to authenticated encryption (AEAD), and know why unauthenticated encryption invites padding-oracle and bit-flipping attacks in practice.

Lecture 9: Modes of Operation for Block Ciphers
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 85 min

A single block cipher call only secures one fixed-size block. ECB, CBC, CFB, OFB, and CTR extend it to arbitrary-length messages by chaining ciphertext or keystream across blocks instead of encrypting each one in isolation.

▶ YouTube
SP 800-38A: Recommendation for Block Cipher Modes of Operation
NIST · confidentiality-only modes: ECB, CBC, CFB, OFB, CTR

The formal specification behind the lecture: exact encryption/decryption equations, IV and counter-block generation requirements, and the explicit statement that none of these five modes provide authentication on their own.

↗ Spec
The ECB Penguin
Filippo Valsorda · 10 Nov 2013

Encrypts a bitmap of the Linux Tux mascot under AES-128-ECB and shows the outline surviving intact, because identical plaintext blocks always encrypt to identical ciphertext blocks — the clearest possible demonstration of why ECB leaks structure.

Build: reproduce it yourself: split a bitmap's header from its pixel data, encrypt the pixel data with AES-128-ECB, reattach the header, and confirm the shape survives; then repeat with CTR mode and confirm it doesn't.
↗ Read
Security Flaws Induced by CBC Padding — Applications to SSL, IPSEC, WTLS
Serge Vaudenay · EUROCRYPT 2002

Shows that a server which merely reports whether decrypted CBC padding is valid gives a chosen-ciphertext attacker enough signal to recover plaintext one byte at a time — the same class of flaw that let 2014's POODLE attack strip SSL 3.0 connections.

↗ Paper
SP 800-38D: Galois/Counter Mode (GCM) and GMAC
NIST · authenticated encryption with associated data

Defines GCM: CTR-mode encryption plus a GHASH-based universal-hash authentication tag computed over ciphertext and associated data in one pass, so decryption fails closed on tampering instead of quietly returning altered plaintext.

↗ Spec
RFC 8439: ChaCha20 and Poly1305 for IETF Protocols
Y. Nir & A. Langley · IETF, June 2018

Specifies the AEAD alternative to GCM used across TLS 1.3, WireGuard, and SSH: a software-friendly stream cipher combined with a one-time Poly1305 MAC via encrypt-then-MAC composition, avoiding GCM's dependence on constant-time hardware AES.

Verify: take one ciphertext/tag pair, flip a single ciphertext bit, and confirm your AEAD library refuses to release any plaintext at all — not even the wrong plaintext — before the tag check passes.
↗ RFC
MODULE 05

Hash functions and MACs

Define the three hash-function security properties (preimage, second-preimage, collision resistance), trace the Merkle-Damgård construction and its length-extension weakness, understand SHA-3/Keccak's sponge construction as the structural fix, then build HMAC and understand why it resists length-extension where a naive hash-based MAC doesn't. Close by separating two jobs a hash function gets pressed into that need opposite properties: HKDF's general-purpose key derivation wants speed, and password hashing wants the opposite — deliberate, memory-hard slowness.

Lecture 20: Hash Functions
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 65 min

Defines preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance, and collision resistance as three separate security properties, then builds the Merkle-Damgård iterated construction that every SHA-1/SHA-2-family hash uses to turn a fixed-size compression function into an arbitrary-length hash.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 21: SHA-1 Hash Function
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 79 min

Walks the compression function and message schedule of a real, deployed Merkle-Damgård hash step by step. SHA-1 itself is deprecated for collision resistance, but SHA-2 (FIPS 180-4) inherits the same iterated structure — and the same length-extension weakness.

▶ YouTube
Flickr's API Signature Forgery Vulnerability
Thai Duong & Juliano Rizzo · advisory MOCB-01, 28 Sep 2009

A real, disclosed and fixed exploit of Merkle-Damgård length extension: Flickr signed API requests with MD5(secret‖message), letting an attacker who sees one valid signature compute a valid signature for secret‖message‖padding‖extra without ever learning the secret.

Attack: given only a hash and the byte length of secret‖message, use a length-extension tool to forge a valid MD5(secret‖message‖pad‖extra) tag, then explain in one paragraph why HMAC blocks exactly this move.
↗ Read
The Next Big SHA? SHA3 Sponge Function Explained
Dr Mike Pound · Computerphile · 28 min

Keccak/SHA-3 (FIPS 202) replaces Merkle-Damgård with a sponge: absorb input into a large permutation state, then squeeze out output, so there's no simple chaining variable left for an attacker to extend from — the structural fix, not a patched workaround.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 25: MAC (Message Authentication Codes) and HMAC
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 75 min

Builds HMAC's nested construction, HMAC(K,m) = H((K⊕opad) ‖ H((K⊕ipad) ‖ m)), and argues why the outer hash call — starting from a fresh, secret-derived chaining value — closes exactly the gap that let the Flickr attack forge a naive H(secret‖message) tag. Formally specified in RFC 2104.

▶ YouTube
SP 800-38B: The CMAC Mode for Authentication
Morris Dworkin · NIST

The block-cipher-based alternative to a hash-based MAC: CMAC runs CBC-MAC with a derived subkey to fix its short-message forgery weakness, giving a MAC built from AES instead of SHA-2 when a design already carries a block cipher but not a hash function.

↗ Spec
RFC 5869: HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function (HKDF)
H. Krawczyk & P. Eronen · IETF, May 2010

Splits key derivation into extract (compress a possibly-biased shared secret, like a raw DH output, into a uniform pseudorandom key via HMAC) then expand (stretch that key into as many independent subkeys as a protocol needs). This is the general-purpose KDF this guide's TLS 1.3 capstone runs its entire key schedule on — and it assumes its input already has enough entropy to look random, an assumption a human password violates completely.

Build: derive three independent subkeys from one shared DH secret using HKDF-Extract then HKDF-Expand with distinct info strings, and confirm none of the three is predictable from the other two without the master secret.
↗ RFC
RFC 9106: Argon2 Memory-Hard Function for Password Hashing and Proof-of-Work Applications
A. Biryukov, D. Dinu, D. Khovratovich & S. Josefsson · IETF, September 2021

A password is low-entropy by definition, so running it through SHA-256 or even HKDF (previous lesson) does nothing to stop an offline attacker from trying billions of guesses per second against stolen hashes. The PBKDF2 → bcrypt → scrypt → Argon2 lineage answers this by making each guess deliberately expensive: PBKDF2 iterates a PRF thousands of times, bcrypt adds a tunable cost factor on top of Blowfish's key schedule, and scrypt (Colin Percival, 2009) first made the function memory-hard so GPUs and ASICs can't parallelize past it cheaply — the property Argon2, winner of the 2015 Password Hashing Competition, formalizes with independent time, memory, and parallelism parameters.

Build: hash the same password under SHA-256, PBKDF2, and Argon2id with realistic parameters; estimate each one's cost against an attacker guessing at 10 billion hashes/second on stolen output, and explain why it's the memory parameter, not the iteration count, that breaks that estimate for Argon2.
↗ RFC

Number theory for public-key cryptography

Build exactly the modular-arithmetic and group-theory vocabulary the next three modules (RSA, Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves) need — modular exponentiation, Euler's theorem/totient function, multiplicative groups mod n, and the discrete logarithm problem as a hardness assumption — without turning into a full number theory course.

Lecture 11: Number Theory for PKC: Euclidean Algorithm, Euler's Phi Function & Euler's Theorem
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 91 min

The extended Euclidean algorithm for modular inverses, Euler's totient function φ(n), and Euler's theorem a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) — the three facts every public-key construction in the next modules quietly assumes.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 12: The RSA Cryptosystem and Efficient Exponentiation
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 88 min

Take the square-and-multiply algorithm from the first part of this lecture only: computing a^e mod n in O(log e) modular multiplications instead of e−1, which is what makes exponentiation-based public-key schemes computationally feasible at all. Lecture 13: Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange and the Discrete Log Problem
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 81 min

Introduces the discrete logarithm problem in (Z/pZ)*: given g and g^x mod p, finding x is believed hard even though computing g^x mod p is easy — the asymmetry the next module's Diffie-Hellman protocol is built on.

Lecture 14: The Generalized Discrete Log Problem and the Security of Diffie-Hellman

MODULE 07

RSA: trapdoor permutations to OAEP

Build RSA from key generation through the RSA problem's relationship to factoring, show why textbook RSA (raw modular exponentiation, no padding) is insecure, and land on OAEP/PSS as the standardized fix — grounded in one real historical RSA implementation failure.

Lecture 12: The RSA Cryptosystem and Efficient Exponentiation
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 88 min

Key generation from two large primes, encryption/decryption as modular exponentiation, and why RSA stays fast enough to use at 2048-bit moduli.

▶ YouTube
A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography: Public Key Tools
Dan Boneh & Victor Shoup · free textbook, Ch. 10, §10.2–10.3

Formalizes RSA as a trapdoor permutation under the RSA assumption and states precisely why breaking RSA is no harder than factoring N — and why the converse has never been proven.

↗ Read
Twenty Years of Attacks on the RSA Cryptosystem
Dan Boneh · Notices of the AMS, 1999 · survey

Raw RSA (no padding) is deterministic and multiplicatively homomorphic: identical plaintexts produce identical ciphertexts, and an attacker holding two ciphertexts can forge a third without ever touching the private key.

↗ Read
RFC 8017: PKCS #1 — RSA Cryptography Specifications Version 2.2
IETF · §7 RSAES-OAEP, §8 RSASSA-PSS

Randomized, hash-based padding that removes textbook RSA's malleability and determinism: OAEP for encryption, PSS for signatures. RSAES-PKCS1-v1.5 stays in the spec only for legacy compatibility.

Build: encrypt one RFC 8017 Appendix test vector under RSAES-OAEP and confirm your output matches the published ciphertext byte for byte.
↗ RFC
Chosen Ciphertext Attacks Against Protocols Based on the RSA Encryption Standard PKCS #1
Daniel Bleichenbacher · CRYPTO 1998 · the original padding-oracle paper

A server that merely reports "padding valid/invalid" on PKCS#1 v1.5 ciphertexts leaks enough to decrypt any intercepted ciphertext in under a million queries — the attack that pushed the field toward OAEP, and that still resurfaces as ROBOT and DROWN whenever an implementation regresses.

Attack: build a toy PKCS#1 v1.5 padding oracle over a small RSA modulus and run Bleichenbacher's adaptive search until you recover a chosen ciphertext's plaintext.
↗ Paper
MODULE 08

Diffie-Hellman, ElGamal, and elliptic curves

Build Diffie-Hellman from the discrete-log hardness assumption, show the man-in-the-middle gap unauthenticated DH leaves open, extend to ElGamal encryption, then move the same ideas onto elliptic curves (ECDH) and see why curve choice — P-256 vs. Curve25519 — matters in practice. Close with the third way to close that man-in-the-middle gap: not a certificate, a shared password.

Lecture 13: Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange and the Discrete Log Problem
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 81 min

Two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel using only modular exponentiation in a cyclic group; security rests entirely on the discrete logarithm being hard to invert.

▶ YouTube
Key Exchange Problems
Dr Mike Pound · Computerphile · 9 min

Plain Diffie-Hellman authenticates nothing: an active attacker who intercepts both exchanges completes two separate key agreements and silently relays (and reads) every message. Boneh & Shoup formalize this as the attack on the anonymous DH protocol (§10.7) — the gap certificates and signatures exist to close.

Attack: script a MITM against a toy DH exchange between two local processes that send (p, g, A) and (p, g, B) in the clear; substitute your own values on both sides and show each party derives a different session key, both known to you.
▶ YouTube
Lecture 15: Elgamal Encryption Scheme
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 78 min

Turns Diffie-Hellman into public-key encryption by masking the plaintext with a shared DH secret; the ciphertext is a fresh ephemeral key plus a masked message, and security reduces to the decisional Diffie-Hellman assumption.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 16: Introduction to Elliptic Curves
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 81 min

Point addition and doubling on a Weierstrass curve over a finite field, built geometrically first and then algebraically, as the group structure every EC scheme reuses.

▶ YouTube
Lecture 17: Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 87 min

Re-derives Diffie-Hellman and ElGamal over the EC group instead of (Z/pZ)*: the same discrete-log hardness assumption, with far smaller keys for equivalent security.

Build: implement ECDH over Curve25519 (X25519) between two local peers; confirm both derive an identical shared secret from their own private scalar and the other party's public point.
▶ YouTube
Curve25519: New Diffie-Hellman Speed Records
Daniel J. Bernstein · original design paper, 2006

Bernstein's stated design goals — a fast, simple, constant-time, misuse-resistant Montgomery curve at the 128-bit security level — against NIST P-256's Solinas-generated Weierstrass curve with an unexplained seed. SafeCurves (also cr.yp.to) scores both against a public rubric instead of a vendor's word.

↗ Read
RFC 9382: SPAKE2, a PAKE
W. Ladd & B. Kaduk · IETF CFRG, February 2023

Plain Diffie-Hellman needs an out-of-band authenticated channel to close the man-in-the-middle gap two lessons back; SPAKE2 gets authentication from a low-entropy shared password instead, by blinding each party's DH share with a password-derived group element so an attacker who doesn't know the password can test at most one guess per interaction — turning an offline dictionary attack into an online one that rate-limiting can actually stop. OPAQUE (Jarecki–Krawczyk–Xu, Eurocrypt 2018) extends the same goal to the asymmetric case, where a server stores no password-equivalent data at all, and is the scheme now moving through IETF standardization for exactly that reason.

Derive: given a SPAKE2 exchange between two toy parties sharing a password, show why an eavesdropper who intercepts one full exchange still can't verify a guessed password offline — then show what breaks if the password-derived blinding element gets reused across sessions instead of fixed per protocol instance.

MODULE 09

Digital signatures, PKI, and TLS 1.3

Survey the real signature-scheme landscape (RSA-PSS, ECDSA, EdDSA) and why nonce reuse is catastrophic for ECDSA specifically, then build up through X.509 certificates and CA chains to TLS 1.3's handshake — the capstone that ties every primitive in this guide into one real, currently-deployed protocol.

Lecture 18: Digital Signatures and Security Services
Christof Paar · Ruhr University Bochum · 77 min

RSA and ElGamal/DSA-style signatures built from the same trapdoor and discrete-log assumptions as their encryption counterparts, plus the security services — authenticity, integrity, non-repudiation — a signature actually buys you.

▶ YouTube
27C3: Console Hacking 2010
bushing, marcan, segher & sven (fail0verflow) · 27C3, Dec 2010 · 44 min

Sony signed every PS3 firmware update with the same ECDSA nonce k. Boneh & Shoup formalize exactly why that's fatal (§19.3): two signatures under one reused k reduce to two linear equations solvable by hand for k and then the private key — the same bug class that later hit some Bitcoin wallets.

Derive: given two ECDSA signatures (r, s1) and (r, s2) over known messages m1, m2 that share a nonce, solve the two congruences by hand for k, then for the private key d.
▶ CCC
High-Speed High-Security Signatures
Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe & Yang · Ed25519 design paper, 2011

Derives the signing nonce deterministically from the private key and the message via a hash, so the same (key, message) pair always produces the same nonce — nonce reuse across distinct messages becomes structurally impossible instead of an operational hope.

↗ Read
RFC 5280: Internet X.509 Public Key Infrastructure Certificate and CRL Profile
IETF · certificate and revocation-list format

A certificate is a signed statement binding a public key to a subject name; §4 gives the field-by-field structure — subject, issuer, validity, SAN, key usage, signature algorithm — before trusting any chain.

Where this leads next

Every module here feeds a specific sibling guide. This is the map from "which primitive just broke or fell short" to "which guide picks it up."

Quantum computer breaks RSA/DH/ECCShor's algorithm factors and solves discrete logs in polynomial time on a large enough quantum computer. Symmetric ciphers and hashes need larger parameters, not new math. See the PQC guide.
Need to compute on encrypted dataTLS and AEAD protect data in transit and at rest, but decrypt before computing. If the evaluator must never see plaintext, see the FHE guide.
Several parties, no shared trustJoint computation over private inputs where no single party learns anyone else's input. See the MPC guide.
Prove a claim without revealing whyA signature proves you hold a key; it doesn't prove an arbitrary statement while hiding the witness. See the ZKP guide.
Ongoing sessions, forward secrecy, groupsTLS 1.3 secures one connection. Ratchets, double-ratchet forward secrecy, and group membership state are the E2E guide.
Untrusted host, not just untrusted networkThis guide assumes the endpoints are trusted and the channel isn't. If the machine running the code is itself the adversary, see the TEE guide.
Shared ordering and settlementSignatures authenticate a message; they don't establish a single agreed history among mutually distrusting parties. See the chain guide.

Capstones and exit criteria

Implementation capstone

  1. Implement AES-128 in software from FIPS 197 alone, no library.
  2. Wrap it in GCM per SP 800-38D.
  3. Run known-answer test vectors and confirm bit-exact output.
  4. Compare timing against an AES-NI hardware path.
  5. Document every place a real deployment would use a vetted library instead of your own code, and why.

Protocol capstone

  1. Generate an RSA or ECDSA keypair and a self-signed certificate.
  2. Capture a real TLS 1.3 handshake to a server using that certificate.
  3. Annotate every field against Module 9's primitive list.
  4. Break one deliberately: reused ECDSA nonce, missing certificate validation, or ECB instead of GCM.
  5. Explain in writing which module's lesson predicts the exact failure.

Weekly loop

  • Watch: separate the construction from the security claim.
  • Derive: reconstruct one equation, reduction, or attack by hand.
  • Build: reproduce one artifact with real test vectors.
  • Break: reuse a nonce, drop a padding check, or flip a bit.
  • Explain: state the assumption the construction actually rests on.

Minimum portfolio

  • LFSR keystream recovery from 2n bits.
  • ECB-penguin and CTR-mode comparison.
  • HMAC vs. naive H(secret‖message) length-extension demo.
  • Argon2id vs. PBKDF2 vs. bare SHA-256 password-hash cost comparison.
  • RSA-OAEP encrypt/decrypt against a published test vector.
  • ECDH over Curve25519 between two local peers.
  • SPAKE2 exchange demonstrating an eavesdropper can't verify an offline password guess.
  • Annotated TLS 1.3 handshake capture.

Exit criteria

Foundations and symmetric primitives
You can state Shannon's perfect-secrecy result and its impracticality, trace one round each of DES and AES by hand, and explain why AES-NI closes a real cache-timing attack.
Modes, hashes, and MACs
You can explain why ECB leaks structure, build an AEAD mode from a block cipher, trace Merkle-Damgård's length-extension weakness, and construct HMAC to close it.
Key derivation and password hashing
You can explain why HKDF's speed and Argon2's deliberate slowness are the correct answer to two different threat models, and estimate the cost gap between a fast hash and a memory-hard one against an offline attacker guessing stolen output.
Public-key primitives
You can derive RSA from the factoring-hardness assumption, explain why textbook RSA needs OAEP/PSS, build Diffie-Hellman and ElGamal from the discrete-log assumption, move the same construction onto elliptic curves, and explain how SPAKE2 gets DH authentication from a shared password instead of a certificate.
Signatures and deployment
You can explain why ECDSA nonce reuse recovers the private key, why EdDSA's deterministic nonce prevents it structurally, and trace a certificate chain from leaf to trust root.
Protocol composition
You can annotate a real TLS 1.3 handshake and name which primitive in this guide produced each field, from key_share to CertificateVerify to Finished.
Place in the eight
You can reject "just use RSA" or "just use AES" when the actual requirement is quantum resistance, computing on ciphertext, joint computation without a trusted party, a hardware-adversary threat model, or session-level forward secrecy — and name the sibling guide that owns each.

Curation policy

Scope: the classical toolbox — symmetric ciphers, hash functions, MACs, key derivation, password hashing, RSA, Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves, password-authenticated exchange, digital signatures, and TLS. Sibling routes: PQC, FHE, MPC, ZKP, DLT, TEE, and E2E. Priority went to the primary designers' own lectures and papers, and to the standards each construction ships as — not secondary explainers once a primary source existed.
