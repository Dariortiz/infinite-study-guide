Stochastic Process Zoo · infinite study guide

Darío's SPZ MOOC

Families first; named creatures second.

A field guide assembled from Robert Gallager's MIT 6.262 point-process and queueing lectures, NPTEL's phase-type and queueing modules from IIT Guwahati, and dedicated lecture series by Magnus Nordborg (coalescent theory, ICTS) and Anupam Kundu (interacting particle systems, ICTS), backed by Daley–Vere-Jones, Bladt–Nielsen, Kyprianou, and Liggett. Each module is a self-contained family — point processes, phase-type distributions, branching, coalescence, diffusions, Lévy processes, interacting particle systems, queues — rather than a single unifying arc; the laws, filtrations, and generators those families are built from are assumed from the STO guide, not re-derived here.

P(N(t)=k) = (λt)^k e^{−λt}/k!
E[s^{Z_n}] = f(f(...f(s)))
E[e^{iξ·X_t}] = e^{tψ(ξ)}

Each family gets its own characteristic object: a Poisson process is fixed by its intensity, a Galton–Watson process by its offspring generating function iterated n times, and a Lévy process by the characteristic exponent ψ in its Lévy–Khintchine formula.

Choose your route

There is no single course that tours point processes, branching, coalescence, diffusions, Lévy jumps, interacting particle systems, and queues at a consistent level of rigor. Gallager's and the NPTEL lecturers' modules supply the discrete and queueing families; Nordborg's and Kundu's dedicated ICTS series carry the same construct-a-generator-and-simulate method into genealogies and many-particle systems.

Jump & Counting

Where this leads next

This guide’s job is recognizing, constructing, and simulating named process families, not building general stochastic-process theory from first principles.

Need the general theoryFiltrations, martingale theory, and generator/semigroup machinery are assumed here, not derived. See the STO guide.

Capstones & exit criteria

A finished route ends in an artifact you can run and a statement you can defend.

Comparative process field guide

  1. Choose four process families.
  2. For each, state state space, filtration, generator or characteristic object.
  3. Implement one exact or controlled simulation.
  4. Write a comparison keyed by dependence, jumps, scaling, and inference.

Approximation bridge

  1. Choose one process and an asymptotic approximation.
  2. State the scaling and topology.
  3. Test finite-size error computationally.
  4. Identify precisely which theorem would justify the bridge.

Weekly loop

  • Watch: name the family's characteristic object — intensity, generating function, generator, or exponent — before touching a formula.
  • Derive: reprove one closure or fixed-point calculation by hand, such as thinning, an extinction probability, or a Lévy triplet decomposition.
  • Build: implement one exact or controlled simulation for that family and check it against a known expectation.
  • Explain: state which theorem, scaling, and topology would justify treating this family as an approximation of another.

Minimum portfolio

  • Poisson process simulator that matches its gap-based and order-statistic constructions (Module 00 · Jump & Counting).
  • Galton–Watson extinction laboratory solving the offspring-generating-function fixed point and checking it against simulated family trees (Module 02 · Branching).
  • Kingman's coalescent simulator reproducing expected time-to-MRCA and total tree length (Module 03 · Coalescence).
  • M/G/1 queue validator confirming Little's theorem, with one heavy-traffic reflected-Brownian-motion comparison (Module 07 · Queues & Limits).

Exit criteria

Point processes and phase-type distributions
You can construct a Poisson process from independent exponential gaps or from order statistics, and represent an absorption time as a finite continuous-time Markov chain via Erlang or Coxian phase-type structure.
Branching and coalescence
You can solve a Galton–Watson extinction probability from its offspring generating function and simulate Kingman's coalescent to recover expected time-to-MRCA and total branch length.
Diffusions and Lévy processes
You can classify a one-dimensional diffusion's boundaries from its scale and speed, and decompose a Lévy process's characteristic exponent into drift, Gaussian, and jump components via the Lévy–Itô decomposition.
Interacting systems and queues
You can build a finite-window exclusion or contact process from a graphical construction and validate an M/G/1 queue simulator against Little's theorem.

Curation policy

Scope: this is a field guide — recognizing, constructing, and simulating named stochastic-process families (point processes, phase-type, branching, coalescence, diffusions, Lévy processes, interacting particle systems, queues). The filtration, martingale, and generator/semigroup theory these families are built from is the STO guide, not re-derived here. The numerical-methods and approximation-error analysis behind every simulation lab is the SIM guide's job. DSZ catalogs this guide's deterministic counterpart — maps, flows, and attractors instead of laws and generators.
